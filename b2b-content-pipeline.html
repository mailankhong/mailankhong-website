Content to drive B2B pipeline

A B2B content marketing strategy aimed at pipeline

Mai-Lan Khong, content engineer. Published 30 September 2026

When content should feed pipeline, I look at three things: who it is written for, the order of the channels, and whether the numbers can see what content does. Most founder content I look at gets the reach and skips the rest.

This page is how I think about each part, from LinkedIn to being found in Google and in AI answers. I can show you how the parts connect. I can't promise what they will bring your company, and nobody honestly can.

"Our content isn't driving pipeline"

The pattern I see most, when I look at founders' LinkedIn profiles: the content is written for an audience instead of a buyer, and nothing leads from a post to a conversation. As I wrote in my post of 12 Mar 2026: "The engagement is real. But the pipeline isn't moving."

In that post I split content in two. Audience content casts wide: it gets likes, follows and views, and the reader thinks "interesting". Pipeline content targets your exact buyer: the reader thinks "they get my problem", and success looks like DMs, calls and booked meetings. Pipeline content is specific and problem-first. "It's not trying to go viral BUT trying to make one person feel seen enough to reach out."

You need both. From the same post: "Both matter. One gets you the room. The other gets you the right conversation in it."

The path from a post to a conversation

Pipeline rarely comes from one post. It comes from a buyer meeting your name again and again, in different places, until the problem shows up. From my post of 28 Aug 2026:

  1. Someone sees your post.
  2. A week later, they see you making a useful comment.
  3. Someone they follow tags you in a conversation.
  4. They click your profile, and they see your work.
  5. "Then few months later, their company has the exact problem you've been talking about."

When that path works, your other channels change as well. Outbound and ads land on a name the buyer already knows. In the team program I run, from my post of 6 Aug 2026, pipeline looks like "the warm DMs from prospects who arrive half-sold before the first call" and "the paid budget going to posts that already won organically, instead of random boosts on the corporate page."

What does B2B content marketing mean?

B2B content marketing is publishing useful content for the people who buy for a business, so the right ones come to know you, trust you, and reach out. The Content Marketing Institute defines content marketing as "a strategic marketing approach focused on creating and distributing valuable, relevant, and consistent content to attract and retain a clearly defined audience".

In B2B, the hard part is "clearly defined". A B2B content marketing strategy starts with who the buyer is and what they are trying to solve, and only then with topics and formats.

Is LinkedIn good for B2B marketing?

In my view, yes, as the first channel: start where the reach already lives.

From the same post: "It's the one channel that hands you reach before anyone knows your name: these days the algorithm matches you to people by topic, not by your follower count."

There is a second reason now. AI answers read LinkedIn too: in my own test before these pages went live, Google's AI answer cited one of my LinkedIn posts (28 Sep 2026). From my post of 7 Sep 2026: "Because the feed has a second reader now and it reads differently." (Section 8 is about that reader.)

LinkedIn is rented ground, and that is fine at the start. From my post of 8 Jun 2026: "Owning your audience is the right goal, but it's the wrong first move."

How do B2B founders generate pipeline from LinkedIn content?

The order I recommend: LinkedIn first to get the audience, then a newsletter you own, then YouTube when you can commit to it. And on LinkedIn, in my own work, the deals I trace back usually started in a comment thread rather than in a post.

The order of channels. From my post of 8 Jun 2026, LinkedIn, a newsletter and YouTube "aren't 3 options you choose between. They're 3 stages, and they work in order."

StageIts jobFrom my post of 8 Jun 2026
1. LinkedInGet found by the right people"You're not there to own, you're there to get found."
2. A newsletterMove them to something you own"A newsletter with no channel feeding it is like speaking to an empty room."
3. YouTubeCompound trust, slowly"it's the only one that keeps compounding trust for years after you hit publish."

Run it backwards and it fails early: "Nothing kills a founder's belief in content faster than a newsletter that sends to 40 people. Mom, two friends, one competitor." And: "Content works but the order was wrong."

This order is my view from the work. I have no results from a newsletter or from YouTube to show you yet.

The comments. From my post of 21 May 2026: "It's wayyyy EASIER to book calls from commenting on LinkedIn than from posting." And from the same post: "almost every time I trace where a deal actually started, it leads back to a comment thread."

The path I described in that post:

  1. A sharp comment that adds something to the post.
  2. Replies in the thread: "reply 3 to 4 times, something new each time".
  3. "let's take this offline".
  4. A warm DM the next day: one real question, no pitch.
  5. A call, ideally.

How do I measure whether LinkedIn content drives pipeline?

Measure what a last-click report cannot see: ask buyers where they heard of you, watch your branded search and your visits from AI answers, and report the deals your content touched, not only the ones it closed.

From my post of 14 Sep 2026:

  1. Ask buyers directly. "Add one field to your demo form, "How did you hear about us?", with an AI assistant option next to Google and LinkedIn, and record it on every won deal."
  2. Watch branded search and AI referrals. "Plot branded searches, direct traffic and visits from chatgpt and perplexity against the weeks you post." Then read it carefully. When the lines climb in the weeks after you start posting, and nothing else changed that month, it points to your content. It is a sign, not proof, and what buyers tell you in step 1 weighs more.
  3. Report influenced pipeline. "Log every LinkedIn touch in your CRM: a profile visit, a comment, a DM, a carousel opened." Then report the deals your content touched next to the ones it closed.

What to stop showing as proof on its own: last-touch attribution, impressions, follower count and the Social Selling Index (LinkedIn's own activity score). They are not useless. They just don't tell you whether content brought in pipeline.

Why is attribution so hard for B2B content?

Because most reports credit the last click, and in B2B, as I see it, much of the decision happens before anyone clicks anything.

This is how it happens, from the same post: "So someone reads your post on the train. They screenshot your carousel into a Slack channel you will never have access to. They Google your name three weeks later and land on your site "directly."" By the time a form is filled, the report gives the credit to Google. AI answers make the gap wider: "a visit from an AI answer carries no record of which post it quoted" (my post of 14 Sep 2026).

Content also doesn't behave like ads, so ad-style attribution misreads it. From my post of 15 Jul 2026: "In ads, you rent a new audience every time you pay." And "Content doesn't work that way. You live in the same room." The same people see you post after post, so you can't find one winning post and run it again and again, the way an ad team does.

How do I write LinkedIn posts that get cited by ChatGPT and AI search?

Put the point in the first line, in the words a buyer would type, name your facts with their dates, and set the post to "Anyone" so it is public. These are the edits from my post of 9 Sep 2026, written after I read the citation studies published this year. Nobody can promise a citation.

"A citation is the moment an AI answer links your post as its source." "It is not a mention of your name and it is not a view."

The edits, from the same post:

  1. Set the post to Anyone, so it is public.
  2. The point in line one, in the words a buyer would type. My own mistake, from the same post: one of my posts opened with a scene, "Founders send me their content strategy fairly often." The sentence an engine could quote, "A calendar answers one question, which is when.", sat in paragraph four.
  3. Name the tool, the number and the date. A vague post has no sentence worth quoting.
  4. Write a list as a list, with bullets or numbers.
  5. Type in plain letters. Bold letters pasted in from a generator are special symbols, not plain letters. As I put it in that post: "The symbols are not the word."
  6. Write it yourself.
  7. Keep posting, so there is recent material to find.
  8. Put the claim in the text, not only in an image. For a carousel, the caption carries the numbers on its slides.

For longer pieces there is one more step. From my post of 7 Sep 2026: "The feed rewards a short post from a person. The machine quotes a long article and prefer a list." So I write it once, then give each place its own copy:

  1. "Write it once for your blog and wait 2 or 3 days, so Google meets the original first."
  2. "Then publish it as a LinkedIn article and cut a post from it for your own profile."
  3. "Then give the company page a version too, so the same idea sits in every container AI reads."

"You could see by this point, what held up is mostly editing, and the human reader loses almost nothing."

This site is part of the same test: I ask AI engines the questions this page answers, before and after it goes live, and I will publish what changes.

How do I know if my company is ready for AI search?

Ask an AI assistant who to consider in your category, then check whether what it would need to name you is public, under a person's name, and says one clear thing.

Start with the test in that post: "You can open ChatGPT and ask it who to hire in your category." Then look at who the answer names and, where it shows its sources, which pages it links to.

Then five checks, from my posts:

  1. Your people publish under their own names, and your company page is in good shape too, because AI answers quote both (16 Sep 2026).
  2. Your posts are public. A post set to connections only is invisible to AI engines (9 Sep 2026).
  3. Your posts add up to one point of view. My test, from my post of 1 Sep 2026: "Someone reads 20 of your posts with your name cut off. Could they explain what you know?"
  4. One category first. "Own one category before you reach for a second" (16 Sep 2026).
  5. Some of your content is written to be quoted: articles as well as posts, lists with real figures inside them, the point in the first line (7 and 16 Sep 2026).

If most of these are missing, start with them. Most of them are editing and publishing work.
